# Model Portfolios

## What a Model Portfolio Is

A model portfolio is a named target allocation that an advisory firm applies to many accounts at once. Instead of deciding the mix for each client individually, the firm's investment team defines a small number of models, each suited to a risk profile or objective, and assigns every managed account to one of them. The model is identified by a firm-specific code, such as a balanced 60/40 model or a liability-driven model, and carries three things: a set of target weights by asset class, a drift tolerance expressed in percentage points, and an effective date from which that definition applies.

## Target Weights by Asset Class

Target weights are expressed as a percentage of the account's total market value and must sum to exactly 100. A typical balanced model might hold 40% US equity, 20% International equity, 35% Core bonds and 5% Cash. Cash is a real asset class with its own target, not a residual; a model that intends to hold no cash says so with a 0% target. Firms may also define target weights at the security level beneath each asset class, but drift and tolerance are measured at the asset-class level, which is the level documented and reported to clients.

## Assigning Accounts to a Model

Each account is assigned to exactly one model at a time. The assignment is made when the account is onboarded, based on the client's risk questionnaire and investment policy, and is recorded with an effective date. Accounts split into sleeves can carry a different model on each sleeve, in which case drift is measured per sleeve. Household members do not have to share a model: a retirement account may follow a conservative model while a taxable account in the same household follows a growth model. The household view simply rolls the actual holdings together for reporting.

## Changing a Model Definition

When the investment team changes a model's target weights, the change is versioned and effective-dated. Accounts on the model keep their existing holdings until the effective date, and on that date drift is recalculated against the new targets. Because the new targets usually put many accounts outside tolerance at once, a model change is normally followed by a coordinated rebalance across every account on the model rather than account-by-account trading. The previous version remains available so that drift reports for earlier dates are reproducible.

## Moving an Account to a Different Model

Moving a single account to another model is a model assignment change, not a model definition change. The new assignment takes effect on a chosen date, and the account is rebalanced toward the new targets from that date. A model reassignment by itself does not change the account's market value; any value change during the transition comes from market movement or trading costs, not from the reassignment.
